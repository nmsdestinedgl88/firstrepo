# MODULE 8 | Allies and Comebacks | From Social Struggles to Real Confidence

## OPENER-1 | Meet the Power
Friendship is where many parents feel the sharpest worry. You may have watched your child get left out, say the wrong thing at the wrong time or melt down after losing a game. It hurts to watch, and it is hard to know how to help.

Kids with ADHD often have wonderful qualities that friends love: humour, energy, loyalty, imagination and generosity. They may also struggle with turn-taking, reading cues and handling disappointment. After a lot of corrections, some children begin to believe they are bad at people or bad at everything.

That belief is the real obstacle. Confidence is built from small experiences of success, from adults who notice effort, and from learning that mistakes are survivable. Every hero has setbacks. What defines them is the comeback.

This final module brings the book together. You will practise friendship skills through play, celebrate mistakes, build a record of wins and finish with a letter that tells your child who they are.

For you, patience here is about believing in the long game. Skills grow slowly. Your steady belief in your child is the thing they carry into every playground, classroom and workplace.

## MISSION 8.1 | The Friendship Recipe | 15 minutes | Paper, pencils

### The Idea
Friendship has ingredients, and kids can learn them like a recipe. In this mission you and your child write a simple Friendship Recipe: the things that make someone a good friend and how to do them.

Include small, specific actions such as saying hello, asking a question, sharing, waiting for a turn and saying sorry. Then act out a few scenes together, such as joining a game that is already going. Practice at home makes the real moment less stressful.

Keep it playful. Take turns playing the friend, and let your child direct the scenes.

### Steps
1. Ask: "What makes someone a good friend?" and list the answers.
2. Pick the three most important ingredients.
3. Turn each into a simple action your child can do.
4. Act out one scene, such as asking to join a game.
5. Swap roles and try again.

### Say This
> "What do good friends do? Let's make a recipe."
> "Let's practise asking to join. I'll be the other kid."

### Notice
- Ingredients your child already knows.
- Moments of embarrassment and how they handled them.
- Which skills feel easy and which feel hard.
- Their sense of humour in the role-play.

### Guardian Pause
If your child makes a social mistake during practice, hold back the correction. Say, "Let's try that again." Playful repetition teaches more than a lecture.

### If It Isn't Working
If role-play feels awkward, use stuffed toys as the characters. If your child resists, talk about a favourite TV show's friendships instead.

## MISSION 8.2 | Turn-Taking Practice | 15 minutes | A simple board or card game

### The Idea
Games are a gentle gym for social skills. Waiting, following rules, winning and losing all come up in a ten-minute game. Practising at home in a safe setting prepares your child for the playground.

Choose a quick game your child enjoys. Name the skills out loud while you play: "I'm waiting for my turn." Model losing well by saying "Good game" and meaning it. Praise effort and kindness, not just winning.

Short games work best. Play two or three rounds and end while it is still fun.

### Steps
1. Pick a short game with clear turns.
2. Before you start, agree on one skill to practice, such as waiting or saying good game.
3. Play and notice the moments when it is hard.
4. When someone loses, say "Good game" and shake hands.
5. Talk afterward about what went well.

### Say This
> "It's your turn in a moment. I'm waiting patiently."
> "Good game. I enjoyed playing with you."

### Notice
- How your child handles waiting.
- Their reaction to losing.
- Moments they showed kindness.
- How you handle losing yourself.

### Guardian Pause
Lose on purpose once or twice and show how you handle it, calmly and with humour. Your reaction is the model they will copy.

### If It Isn't Working
If losing leads to a meltdown, choose cooperative games where you work as a team. Stop the game early if emotions rise, and try again later.

## MISSION 8.3 | Oops Olympics | 10 minutes | Paper, a medal made of foil or card

### The Idea
Mistakes are part of learning, but many kids with ADHD feel them sharply. They have heard "careless" and "silly" too often. The Oops Olympics flips the script and celebrates mistakes as proof of trying.

Each person names a recent mistake, says what they learned and receives a pretend medal. Share your own first. When children see adults laugh at their errors and learn from them, mistakes feel less shameful.

Keep the tone warm and lighthearted, never mocking.

### Steps
1. Make simple medals from card and string or foil.
2. Take turns sharing a recent oops, big or small.
3. For each, ask: "What did you learn?"
4. Award a medal and a cheer.
5. Start a "lessons learned" list in this book.

### Say This
> "My oops today was burning the toast. I learned to set a timer."
> "Gold medal for trying! What did you learn?"

### Notice
- How your child reacts to sharing a mistake.
- What they say they learned.
- Whether they become more willing to try new things.
- Your own feelings about sharing mistakes.

### Guardian Pause
Resist fixing or lecturing during the ceremony. The medal is the message. If you feel a lecture rising, take a breath and cheer instead.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child won't share, start with your own oops until they feel safe. If they say "I don't make mistakes," smile and share another of yours.

## MISSION 8.4 | The Brag Board | 15 minutes | A board, paper, pens

### The Idea
Many kids with ADHD hear far more correction than praise. The Brag Board redresses the balance with a visible record of what they have done well. Wins can be big or small: finishing a page of reading, being kind to a sibling, trying a new food, staying calm in a tough moment.

The praise that works best is specific. "Great job" slides past. "You waited your turn even though you wanted to go first" lands. Specific praise tells your child exactly what to repeat.

Add to the board every day, and read it aloud on hard days.

### Steps
1. Choose a board, wall space or large sheet of paper.
2. Let your child decorate it and give it a name.
3. Each day, write one specific win on a sticky note or card.
4. Ask your child to add their own wins, too.
5. Read the board together once a week.

### Say This
> "I noticed you stayed calm when your sister took your toy. That took strength."
> "What's one thing you're proud of this week?"

### Notice
- How your child responds to specific praise.
- Wins they notice themselves.
- Whether the board lifts their mood.
- How hard or easy it is for you to find wins on a rough day.

### Guardian Pause
On a tough day, look hard for one true thing to praise. Finding it shifts your own mood, too. Strengths are always there, even when behaviour is not at its best.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child dismisses praise, keep it short and factual, without gushing. If they cannot think of wins, offer a few choices and let them pick.

## MISSION 8.5 | The Hero Letter | 20 minutes | Paper, pen, a quiet moment

### The Idea
This final mission is a gift. Write your child a letter that names who they are, what you see in them and what you hope for them. Read it together, or leave it where they can find it.

A letter can be kept and returned to on hard days. It is evidence from the person who knows them best that they are loved, capable and seen.

Take your time. Use real examples from the missions in this book. Be honest, specific and warm.

### Steps
1. Find a quiet time to write alone.
2. Begin with: "Dear [name], here are the things I see in you."
3. Include three strengths with a real example of each.
4. Add one moment from this book that made you proud.
5. End with a sentence of love and belief, and give it to your child.

### Say This
> "I wrote something for you. Would you like me to read it, or read it yourself?"
> "I wrote this because I see how special you are."

### Notice
- How your child reacts as you read.
- What they ask about afterward.
- Where they keep the letter.
- How it feels to write it.

### Guardian Pause
Write a short letter to yourself, too. Name three things you did well as a parent this month. You are the Hero Guardian, and you deserve to be seen as well.

### If It Isn't Working
If writing is hard, record a voice message or draw a picture with captions. If your child is not ready to hear it, tuck the letter away for a day when they are.
