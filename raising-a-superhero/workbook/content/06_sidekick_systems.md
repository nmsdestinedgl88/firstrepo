# MODULE 6 | Sidekick Systems | From Forgetfulness to Reliable Routines

## OPENER-1 | Meet the Power
Every hero has a sidekick, a trusted helper who covers the gaps. For many kids with ADHD, the best sidekick is not a person at all. It is a system.

Forgotten lunchboxes, lost shoes, half-finished chores and endless reminders are everyday life in many homes. It can feel as though you have said the same thing a thousand times. It helps to remember that remembering is hard when attention is busy elsewhere. Your child is not ignoring you on purpose.

Where memory slips, structure can help. Visual routines, set places for things and small steps all take pressure off the brain. A checklist on the wall does the remembering so your child's mind is free for other things.

And here is the strength inside the struggle: when children help design their own systems, they build planning and problem-solving skills they will use all their lives. Kids who have learned to build their own sidekick systems often become adults who are resourceful and organised in their own way.

This module is practical. Each mission sets up one small system. Start with just one or two, and give them time to settle before adding more.

For you, patience means trading reminders for routines. It takes more effort at first, but it saves you from being the human alarm clock.

## MISSION 6.1 | The Morning Mission Board | 20 minutes | Paper, markers or photos

### The Idea
A visual routine shows the steps of the morning in order, so no one has to repeat them. Your child can check off each step as they go, and you can point to the board instead of nagging.

Make the board together. Use drawings, photos of your child doing each step or simple words. Keep it to four to six steps. Letting your child choose the pictures builds ownership.

Put it where it will be seen, such as the bedroom door or the bathroom wall.

### Steps
1. List the morning steps with your child, such as get dressed, eat, brush teeth, pack bag.
2. Draw or photograph each step on a card or paper.
3. Arrange them in order on a board or wall.
4. Add a checkbox or a movable marker for each step.
5. Try it tomorrow and adjust anything that did not work.

### Say This
> "What's the first step on your board?"
> "You did it without me saying anything. Nice work."

### Notice
- Which steps are quickest and which take longest.
- Whether pointing to the board replaces reminders.
- How your child feels about checking items off.
- The steps that need extra support.

### Guardian Pause
When your child gets stuck, point to the board instead of speaking. Waiting even five seconds before stepping in gives your child a chance to take the next move on their own.

### If It Isn't Working
If the board is ignored, shrink it to three steps or add a small reward at the end. Check that the order makes sense to your child, and let them rearrange it.

## MISSION 6.2 | The Launch Pad | 15 minutes | A hook, a basket, a shelf

### The Idea
Items go missing when they have no home. A launch pad is a dedicated spot near the door where everything for tomorrow gets placed the night before: bag, shoes, jacket, library book, water bottle.

Setting it up together turns "Where are my shoes?" into "They're on the launch pad." It reduces the morning scramble and gives your child a sense of control.

Label the spots with pictures if needed and keep the pad clear of everything else.

### Steps
1. Pick a spot near the front door.
2. Add hooks, a basket and a shelf at your child's height.
3. Label each place with a picture or word.
4. Practise the night-before routine together.
5. Do a quick "launch check" before leaving in the morning.

### Say This
> "Everything for tomorrow goes on the launch pad."
> "Run a launch check. Is anything missing?"

### Notice
- Items that tend to disappear.
- Whether your child uses the pad without reminders.
- How mornings feel with a launch pad in place.
- What would make it easier to use.

### Guardian Pause
When something lands in the wrong place, resist the urge to sigh. Gently say, "Where does that live?" and let your child move it. Consistency beats perfection.

### If It Isn't Working
If the pad becomes a dumping ground, do a weekly reset together. If your child still forgets, add a reminder picture to the door.

## MISSION 6.3 | One-Step Cards | 15 minutes | Index cards, markers

### The Idea
"Clean your room" is not one task. It is ten. For a child who is easily overwhelmed, that one sentence can feel impossible. Breaking a big task into small steps on separate cards makes it manageable.

Write each step on a card, such as "Put books on the shelf" and "Put dirty clothes in the basket." Your child picks up one card at a time, does it and moves it to a finished pile. The cards show progress, which is motivating.

Start with a task that is not too heavy.

### Steps
1. Choose a task such as tidying a bedroom or packing a bag.
2. Write each tiny step on its own card with your child.
3. Put the cards in a stack, face down or in order.
4. Your child takes one card, does the step and puts it in the done pile.
5. Celebrate when the stack is finished.

### Say This
> "Let's break it into tiny steps. What's the first one?"
> "Look at that done pile. You've finished six steps."

### Notice
- Which steps are hardest.
- How your child reacts to seeing the stack shrink.
- Whether they need a break halfway.
- How much less you needed to say.

### Guardian Pause
Resist the urge to point out what is not done. Notice each completed card out loud. Calm encouragement keeps the effort alive.

### If It Isn't Working
If it still feels overwhelming, start with only three cards. For older kids, use a list on paper they can cross out. Let them design the cards.

## MISSION 6.4 | Beat the Clock, Friendly | 10 minutes | A visual timer

### The Idea
Time is invisible, which makes it hard to manage. A visual timer that shows time shrinking turns it into something you can see. It lets your child pace themselves, and it gives you a neutral way to measure instead of repeating "hurry up."

Use it as a friendly challenge, not a threat. The aim is to see how much can be done before the timer ends, with you cheering rather than pressuring.

Pair it with a short, specific task like putting away toys.

### Steps
1. Choose a visual timer, sand timer or phone countdown.
2. Pick a short task and estimate the time it needs.
3. Set the timer and start together.
4. Cheer progress as the time shrinks.
5. When it rings, celebrate whatever got done.

### Say This
> "Let's see how much we can do before the timer ends."
> "You beat your own record. Nice."

### Notice
- Whether the visual timer works better than spoken time warnings.
- How your child reacts when it runs out.
- Whether they begin to estimate time more accurately.
- The tasks that suit timing and those that do not.

### Guardian Pause
Stay beside your child as a cheerleader and work on something of your own at the same time. When you are calm and busy, the task feels less like a standoff.

### If It Isn't Working
If the timer causes stress, switch to a gentle melody that plays until a task is done, or time yourself instead. Keep the stakes low.

## MISSION 6.5 | The Evening Reset | 10 minutes | A short checklist

### The Idea
A five-minute reset at the end of the day makes tomorrow easier. It might include a quick tidy of the main living space, checking the launch pad and looking at tomorrow's plan.

This mission ties the other systems together. Keep it short and predictable, and do it at the same time each evening. Over time it becomes part of the family rhythm, and your mornings should feel calmer.

Make it a team effort where everyone has a job.

### Steps
1. Pick a regular time, such as just after dinner.
2. Agree on three to four resets: tidy the living room, check the launch pad, lay out clothes.
3. Put them on a short checklist.
4. Set a five-minute timer and do them together.
5. End by looking at tomorrow's plan.

### Say This
> "Time for our reset. What's first on the list?"
> "Thanks for doing that. Tomorrow will be easier."

### Notice
- Which resets your child does most easily.
- Mornings after a reset versus without.
- How you feel at the end of the day.
- What you might drop to keep it simple.

### Guardian Pause
Let go of perfection. The living room does not need to look like a magazine. Five good-enough minutes are better than a long evening of nagging.

### If It Isn't Working
If it drags on, cut the list in half. If evenings are too tired, move the reset earlier. Pick the shortest version you can keep up.
