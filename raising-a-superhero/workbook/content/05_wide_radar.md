# MODULE 5 | Wide Radar | From Distractibility to Curiosity

## OPENER-1 | Meet the Power
You ask your child to put on their shoes. Five minutes later they are at the window watching a bird, with one shoe on and a long story about the bird's nest.

It is easy to read this as not listening. Another reading is that your child's attention is wide open. They notice the bird, the new sound from next door, the loose thread on your sleeve. Their radar picks up everything.

A wide radar can be a real strength. People who notice a lot make good artists, scientists, detectives, first responders and storytellers. They spot what others miss. Curiosity can shape careers and friendships for a lifetime.

The difficulty is that radar does not come with a filter. When a task is dull or the room is busy, it is hard to choose what to focus on. Instructions get lost. Time slips by.

This module treats noticing as a skill to be proud of, and then gives it a direction. Your child practises paying attention on purpose, in short and playful bursts. They learn that curiosity is welcome, and that they can steer it.

For you, patience means making room for detours. Not every tangent needs stopping. Some can be followed for five minutes and turned into learning.

PULLQUOTE: A child who notices everything has the makings of a very good observer.

## MISSION 5.1 | The Five Senses Walk | 15 minutes | Outdoors or a room

### The Idea
Take your child's wide radar for a walk. A five senses walk is an easy way to turn noticing into a game. You are not testing them. You are exploring together.

Walk around the garden, the street or even the house. Stop at each sense and share what you find: something you see, hear, feel, smell and, if safe, taste. The aim is to show that paying attention can be interesting, not just a demand.

Keep it short and end while it is still fun.

### Steps
1. Choose a place to walk, inside or out.
2. Start with sight: "Find three things you've never noticed before."
3. Move on to sound, touch and smell, one at a time.
4. Take turns sharing what you notice.
5. Finish by asking: "What was your favourite discovery?"

### Say This
> "Let's be explorers. What can you hear right now?"
> "I never noticed that before. Good eye."

### Notice
- Details your child spots that you missed.
- Which sense they enjoy most.
- How long they stay engaged.
- How you feel when you slow down and notice too.

### Guardian Pause
Slow your own pace. Let your child lead when they stop to look at something. Practising presence yourself will make you more patient with their wandering.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child rushes, give a specific challenge like "Find something rough." If they tire quickly, shorten the walk to one sense per day.

## MISSION 5.2 | Notice and Name | 10 minutes | A busy picture or a room

### The Idea
This is a variation on "I spy" with a focus on detail. You ask your child to look carefully and describe what they see, using specific words: colours, shapes, positions and how things might feel.

The game builds the habit of looking closely and putting thoughts into words, which supports listening and storytelling later. It also lets your child shine at something they are naturally good at.

Use a picture book page, a photo or the room you are sitting in.

### Steps
1. Choose a busy picture or look around the room.
2. Take turns saying "I notice..." and naming one detail.
3. Challenge each other to find details that are small, hidden or unusual.
4. Count how many you can find in two minutes.
5. Celebrate with a high five.

### Say This
> "I notice something blue under the table. What do you notice?"
> "How did you spot that? I missed it."

### Notice
- The kinds of details your child chooses.
- Whether they use descriptive words.
- How competitive or cooperative they are.
- Your own habit of skimming past details.

### Guardian Pause
Let your child win some rounds. Take a breath and notice how it feels to be impressed by them. Their strength is real.

### If It Isn't Working
If the game turns into a squabble, play together as a team against the clock. If it is too easy, add rules like only things that are round.

## MISSION 5.3 | Tangent Time | 10 minutes | A timer, curiosity

### The Idea
Tangents are not always distractions. They are often where learning happens. Instead of fighting them, schedule them. Tangent Time is a short window each day when your child may follow any question or idea, with you as a willing guide.

Setting a limit helps both of you. Your child knows their curiosity is welcome. You know when it ends. Many families find that when tangents have a place, they interrupt less.

Use a timer and be present for the whole stretch.

### Steps
1. Agree on a daily Tangent Time, ten minutes is plenty.
2. Ask your child to choose a question they are wondering about.
3. Look it up, draw it, act it out or talk about it.
4. Stop when the timer rings, and write the question in a notebook to revisit.
5. Ask a follow-up the next day.

### Say This
> "It's Tangent Time. What are you curious about?"
> "Great question. Let's save the rest for tomorrow."

### Notice
- Topics your child chooses.
- How they respond when the timer ends.
- Whether tangents during the rest of the day reduce.
- Questions you did not know the answer to.

### Guardian Pause
When a tangent pops up at a bad moment, say "Save it for Tangent Time" calmly and mean it. Practise offering that response without a sigh.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child struggles to stop, use a gentle landing from Module 1. If they cannot pick a topic, offer a jar of question slips to draw from.

## MISSION 5.4 | Spot the Change | 10 minutes | Small household items

### The Idea
Spot the Change is a memory and observation game. You arrange a small collection of items, your child studies it, closes their eyes and you change one thing. They have to spot what is different.

The game practises focused attention in a playful, low-stakes way. It also gives you a chance to see how your child's attention works at its best.

Start easy, with five items and one change, and build up gradually.

### Steps
1. Arrange five small items on a tray or table.
2. Give your child ten seconds to look carefully.
3. Ask them to close their eyes while you change one thing.
4. Let them open their eyes and find the change.
5. Swap roles and let them be the one who changes it.

### Say This
> "Look carefully. I'm going to change something."
> "You found it. What clue did you notice?"

### Notice
- How quickly they spot changes.
- Strategies they use, such as scanning left to right.
- Their delight when they trick you.
- Whether they get discouraged if they miss one.

### Guardian Pause
When you are the one guessing, let yourself get it wrong sometimes. Laugh at your mistakes. Showing that errors are fun can lighten the room for everyone.

### If It Isn't Working
If it is too hard, use fewer items or make the change obvious. If it is too easy, add more items or change two things.

## MISSION 5.5 | Radar to Mission | 10 minutes | Your daily life

### The Idea
The best use of a wide radar is to help others. This mission turns noticing into kindness. Ask your child to spot one person or thing that needs help today, then do something small about it.

It might be a neighbour's dropped letter, a sibling who looks sad, a plant that needs water or a pet that has no fresh water. Kids who feel helpful feel capable, and capable kids are more willing to try hard things.

Celebrate the noticing as much as the helping.

### Steps
1. In the morning, say: "Today your mission is to spot someone or something that needs help."
2. Let your child choose what they notice.
3. Help them decide on one small action.
4. Do it together or let them do it alone.
5. In the evening, ask how it felt.

### Say This
> "Your radar is great at finding things. What did you notice today?"
> "You saw that and helped. That's a hero move."

### Notice
- What your child chooses to notice.
- How they respond to helping.
- Their mood afterward.
- Chances for more helping in the week ahead.

### Guardian Pause
Name the strength out loud: "You noticed. That's a real skill." Hearing it said calmly and specifically matters more than any reward.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child cannot find anything, point them in a direction like "I think the cat might be hungry." Over time they will find their own. Never force it.
