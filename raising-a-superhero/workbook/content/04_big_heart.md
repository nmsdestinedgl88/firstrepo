# MODULE 4 | Big Heart | From Big Feelings to Big Heart

## OPENER-1 | Meet the Power
Joy that fills the room. Anger that arrives like a thunderclap. Tears over a broken crayon. Many kids with ADHD feel things strongly and show them quickly, with little filter between the feeling and the reaction.

This can be hard to live with. You may find yourself walking on eggshells or wondering whether the next outburst is around the corner. You may also feel guilt after you lose your own temper in response.

There is another side. Strong feelings often come with strong empathy. Many of these children are the first to notice when a friend is sad, the first to hug a hurt pet, the loudest in their love. A big heart is a gift to the people around it.

The challenge is helping the feelings settle into words and actions your child can manage. That is a skill, and skills are learned slowly, through repetition and kindness. It is not a matter of willpower, and shame makes it harder.

This module gives you simple ways to build feelings vocabulary, make a calm space and repair after a rough moment. Your child learns that feelings are information, not enemies.

For you, this is the patience heartland. Calm is contagious. When you stay steady, your child borrows your steadiness until they can build their own.

PULLQUOTE: Calm is contagious. Lend yours first.

## MISSION 4.1 | Feelings Weather Report | 5 minutes a day | Paper, markers

### The Idea
Weather is something kids understand. It changes, it can be sunny or stormy, and nobody is to blame for it. Describing feelings as weather makes them easier to talk about and less scary.

Once a day, ask your child for a weather report. "Sunny with a few clouds." "Thunderstorm in my tummy." Then offer yours. Over days, you both build a habit of noticing feelings before they explode.

Keep it light and brief. There are no wrong answers.

### Steps
1. Draw or print simple weather symbols: sun, cloud, rain, storm, rainbow.
2. At a regular time, ask: "What's your feeling weather today?"
3. Let your child point or say it, with no judgment.
4. Share your own weather honestly.
5. Ask one gentle question: "Is there anything that would help the weather?"

### Say This
> "What's your inside weather right now?"
> "Mine is cloudy with a little sunshine. What helps you on stormy days?"

### Notice
- Whether your child chooses words or pictures.
- Patterns in the weather across the week.
- How your child responds when you share yours.
- Which times are sunnier or stormier.

### Guardian Pause
When your child reports a storm, resist the urge to fix it or talk them out of it. Take a breath and say, "Thanks for telling me." Naming comes before solving.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child shrugs, offer two choices: "More sunny or more stormy?" Or draw faces instead of weather. Keep it short and try again tomorrow.

## MISSION 4.2 | Feeling Detective | 15 minutes | Paper, pencils

### The Idea
Many children feel a lot but have few words for it. "Mad" gets used for frustrated, disappointed, embarrassed and worried. A larger vocabulary helps them tell you what is really going on.

In this mission you become detectives together. Look for clues about feelings in faces, in stories and in your own day. Name the clue and the feeling, then guess what might help.

You are not diagnosing. You are collecting words.

### Steps
1. Draw four or five simple faces together, such as happy, sad, angry, nervous and proud.
2. Add a new feeling word to each: excited, disappointed, frustrated, embarrassed.
3. Look at a book, show or photo and ask what the character might feel.
4. Ask: "What clues tell you that?"
5. Share a time you felt one of the feelings.

### Say This
> "What clues tell you he's feeling that way?"
> "Have you ever felt like that? What did it feel like in your body?"

### Notice
- Feelings words your child already knows.
- Where in the body they say feelings show up.
- How willing they are to connect to their own life.
- Words that become part of your family vocabulary.

### Guardian Pause
Share a small, honest moment from your own day, like feeling rushed or disappointed. Modelling that adults have feelings too is powerful. Keep it calm and short.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child resists, make it a guessing game about characters and leave their own feelings out for now. Or use emoji or a feelings poster as a visual aid.

## MISSION 4.3 | The Calm Corner Kit | 20 minutes | A box or basket, small items

### The Idea
A calm corner is a place to reset, never a punishment. It gives your child a safe spot to settle big feelings, with tools they chose themselves. When the corner is built on a good day, it is far easier to use on a bad one.

Fill a box with soothing items: a soft toy, a stress ball, headphones, paper and crayons, a bottle of glitter water, a few calming cards. Let your child decorate it.

Practise using it together before you need it.

### Steps
1. Choose a quiet corner or a spot that feels safe.
2. Give your child a box or basket and invite them to choose what goes inside.
3. Add a card listing three calming steps they like.
4. Practice a pretend big feeling and use the corner together.
5. Agree that either of you may use it when needed.

### Say This
> "This is your calm place. You decide what goes in it."
> "Would you like to go to your corner, or would you like me to sit with you?"

### Notice
- Items your child picks.
- How long they stay in the corner when calm.
- Whether they use it on their own over time.
- How it feels for you to use it, too.

### Guardian Pause
Make yourself a mini Calm Corner Kit. One small item that settles you, like a scented hand cream or a favourite song, can help you stay steady during hard moments.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child avoids the corner, make sure it never feels like a time-out. Invite them to use it with you at first. Keep it free of screens if possible.

## MISSION 4.4 | Breathing Games | 10 minutes | Your own two lungs

### The Idea
Slow breathing is a simple tool that nearly anyone can learn. For children, it works best as a game with a silly name. When a big feeling arrives, a familiar breathing game can give the body a chance to settle.

Try a few and let your child choose the favourites. Practise when things are calm so the skill is ready when needed.

Think of it as a pause button. A slow breath out gives you both a moment to choose what happens next.

### Steps
1. Try "smell the flower, blow the candle": a slow breath in through the nose, a slow breath out through the mouth.
2. Try "balloon belly": place a hand on the tummy and watch it rise and fall.
3. Try "five-finger breathing": trace each finger as you breathe in and out.
4. Pick your favourite and practise three rounds together.
5. Agree on a signal that means "let's breathe."

### Say This
> "Let's smell the flower and blow the candle."
> "Which breathing game do you want to use today?"

### Notice
- Which game your child picks first.
- Whether they can slow their breathing.
- Giggles or silliness, which are fine.
- Whether you feel calmer, too.

### Guardian Pause
Breathe along with your child, and make your out-breath slower than your in-breath. The best time to practise is when you do not need it, so these two minutes are a gift to your future self.

### If It Isn't Working
If breathing feels hard or your child gets dizzy, stop and do something else, like squeezing a cushion. If they find it boring, add a toy to watch rise and fall.

## MISSION 4.5 | Repair and Reconnect | 10 minutes | A calm moment after an upset

### The Idea
Every parent loses their cool sometimes. Every child has meltdowns. What matters most is what happens afterward. Repair is the act of coming back together after a rupture.

Wait until everyone is calm. Then reconnect: acknowledge what happened, take responsibility for your part, listen and say what you will try next time. Repair teaches kids that relationships survive hard moments, and that mistakes can be fixed.

You do not need perfect words. Warmth matters more.

### Steps
1. Wait until you and your child are calm.
2. Offer a hug, a sit-down or an activity, whatever suits them.
3. Say what you noticed: "Earlier we both got upset."
4. Own your part: "I raised my voice. I'm sorry."
5. Ask: "What could help next time?" and listen.

### Say This
> "I'm sorry I yelled. I want to do better. Can we try again?"
> "What do you need from me when you feel like that?"

### Notice
- How your child responds to an apology.
- Ideas they offer for next time.
- How your own body feels after repairing.
- Whether the relationship feels warmer.

### Guardian Pause
Say sorry without a "but." "I'm sorry I yelled" is complete. Add nothing that sounds like an excuse. Notice how it feels to let your shoulders down.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child is not ready to talk, say "I'm here when you're ready" and leave it. Offer repair through action, such as making a snack or sitting nearby. Try again later.
