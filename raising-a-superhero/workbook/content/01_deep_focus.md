# MODULE 1 | Deep Focus | From Hyperfocus to Deep Focus

## OPENER-1 | Meet the Power
Watch your child when they are building, drawing, gaming or hunting for bugs, and you may see something remarkable. The room fades. The noise fades. They go all in.

Many kids with ADHD can focus intensely on things that fascinate them. It can look like a contradiction. How can a child who cannot sit through a worksheet spend three hours on a Lego city? The usual answer is interest. When something is new, exciting or deeply meaningful, attention locks on. When it is not, attention slips.

That is not laziness, and it is not a choice. It is simply how attention often works for these kids. The good news is that it points to something valuable. Deep focus is the engine behind mastery. People who become really good at anything, from chess to coding to cooking, have it.

The challenge is steering. Deep focus does not always arrive on schedule, and it is hard to switch off. Many parents know the sound of "just five more minutes" and the storm that follows when time is up.

This module has two goals. First, help you discover what lights your child up. Second, help your child use that spark to build real skill while learning smoother ways to start and stop.

For you, the Hero Guardian, the work is patience at the edges. Transitions are where most meltdowns live. A little planning, a calm voice and a gentle landing make them far easier.

PULLQUOTE: The goal is not to cut the spark. It is to give it a runway.

## MISSION 1.1 | The Interest Inventory | 15 minutes | Paper, pens, a comfy spot

### The Idea
You cannot steer a spark you have not found. This mission is a relaxed conversation to map what your child loves, without any pressure to be "useful" or "age appropriate."

Ask with real curiosity, not as a quiz. Kids can tell the difference. Interests that seem silly, like a particular video game or a type of slime, are worth writing down. Behind every interest sit skills your child is already building.

When you are done, you will have a list you can come back to throughout this book.

### Steps
1. Sit somewhere relaxed, side by side rather than face to face.
2. Say you are making a list of everything they love. Let them go first.
3. Write down every answer without judgment, even if it surprises you.
4. Ask one follow-up for each: "What do you like most about that?"
5. Circle the top three together.

### Say This
> "I'm making a list of the things you love most. What's on it?"
> "Tell me more about that. What's the best part?"

### Notice
- Which topics make your child's voice speed up or eyes light up.
- Interests that repeat across the list.
- Anything you did not know they cared about.
- Whether they loosened up as the list grew.

### Guardian Pause
Before you start, take one slow breath out, longer than the breath in. Tell yourself: "For fifteen minutes, I am only here to listen." If you notice yourself wanting to steer, let it go and keep listening.

### If It Isn't Working
Some kids freeze when asked what they love. Try a walk through the house instead. Point at objects and ask, "Do you like this?" Or watch what they choose for a few days and write down what you see.

## MISSION 1.2 | Spark to Skill | 15 minutes | Paper, one chosen interest

### The Idea
Now take one interest from your list and ask a question that turns play into growth: "What could you get better at?" A child who loves drawing animals could learn to draw one new animal a week. A child who loves a video game could learn to design a level on paper.

The point is not to make play into schoolwork. It is to show your child that their passion can lead somewhere. Kids who feel good at something carry that feeling into harder areas.

Keep the project tiny so it feels easy to begin. One small skill is plenty.

### Steps
1. Choose one interest from your list.
2. Ask: "What is one thing about this you would like to get better at?"
3. Break it into three small steps together.
4. Choose the first step and do it right now.
5. Put the plan somewhere visible, such as the fridge.

### Say This
> "What's one thing you'd like to learn how to do with this?"
> "What would the first tiny step be?"

### Notice
- Whether your child chooses a goal on their own or needs ideas.
- How they react to a small, finish-able step.
- Pride when the first step is done.
- Any resistance, and what it might be telling you.

### Guardian Pause
If your child picks a goal that seems too big or too odd, pause before you reply. Count to three silently, then ask "How could we make the first step smaller?" Your calm response teaches planning more than any lecture.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child lacks ideas, offer two options and let them pick. Choice builds buy-in. If the goal fizzles after a day or two, that is normal. Try a different interest. The skill being built is trying.

## MISSION 1.3 | The Gentle Landing | 10 minutes | A timer you can see

### The Idea
Hard stops are hard for deeply focused kids. Their brains are fully inside the activity, and a sudden "Time's up!" can feel like being yanked out of a dream.

A gentle landing replaces the yank with a runway. You give warnings, you make the ending predictable, and you tell your child what comes next. Many families find that tantrums shrink when transitions become routine rather than ambush.

Practice on a calm day first, with something low stakes. Then use it when it matters.

### Steps
1. Choose a regular transition, such as screen time ending or leaving a game.
2. Give three warnings: ten minutes, five minutes and one minute.
3. Use a visual timer or a phone alarm they can see.
4. At the one-minute warning, say what comes next: "Then we wash hands for dinner."
5. When time is up, thank them for stopping. Say "Next time you can pick up right here."

### Say This
> "Ten more minutes, then we'll wrap up."
> "Time to save your place. You can come back tomorrow."

### Notice
- Which warning your child responds to best.
- Whether a visual timer helps more than your voice.
- The difference between a landing with a plan and a landing without.
- How you feel when the landing goes well.

### Guardian Pause
At the moment the timer rings, take a breath before you speak. Lower your voice slightly. A quieter tone is often easier to hear than a louder one, and it keeps you steady.

### If It Isn't Working
If warnings are ignored, stand close and make eye contact for the last one. If your child melts down anyway, stay calm, keep the limit and skip the lecture. Talk about it later when you are both settled.

## MISSION 1.4 | Teach Me Time | 10 minutes | Your full attention

### The Idea
Flip the roles. For ten minutes, your child becomes the teacher and you become a curious student. Kids with ADHD often hear what they are doing wrong all day. This mission lets them be the expert.

Teaching is also one of the best ways to understand something deeply. To explain a favourite topic, your child has to organise their thoughts, find words and check whether you are following.

Ask real questions. Resist the urge to correct or add your own facts.

### Steps
1. Pick one of your child's top interests.
2. Say you would love to learn about it, and ask them to teach you for ten minutes.
3. Put your phone away and face them.
4. Ask three genuine questions, such as "Why does it work like that?"
5. End by telling them one thing you learned.

### Say This
> "I know almost nothing about this. Can you teach me?"
> "That's so interesting. What happens next?"

### Notice
- How your child's posture changes when they are the expert.
- Words and ideas they use that surprise you.
- Moments they check that you understand.
- How it feels to simply listen.

### Guardian Pause
If your mind wanders to the to-do list, gently bring it back. Notice the thought, then return to your child's face. Attention is something you can practice, too.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child gets restless after two minutes, shorten it to a quick lightning round of three questions. Or let them show you rather than tell you. Demonstrating counts as teaching.

## MISSION 1.5 | Focus Fort | 15 minutes | A quiet corner, a small timer

### The Idea
A fort is more than a place to play. A defined space with a clear purpose can help a child settle into focus. In this mission you and your child set up a "mission base," a small spot with fewer distractions, then use it for a short stretch of an interest-based project.

The fort can be a blanket over chairs, a corner with cushions or a desk turned away from the busiest part of the room. What matters is that your child helps design it so it feels like theirs.

Keep sessions short at first, ten to fifteen minutes, and end on a high note.

### Steps
1. Let your child choose the spot and gather supplies.
2. Remove obvious distractions together: toys, screens, clutter.
3. Set a visible timer for ten to fifteen minutes.
4. Let your child work on a chosen project from Mission 1.2.
5. When the timer ends, celebrate what got done.

### Say This
> "This is your mission base. What do you need in here?"
> "Look at everything you finished. Nice work."

### Notice
- What helped your child settle in.
- How long they stayed with it.
- Signs of pride when time was up.
- What you might change next time.

### Guardian Pause
While your child works, resist the urge to hover or correct. Stay nearby doing something quiet. Your calm presence is part of the fort.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child cannot settle, shrink the timer to five minutes or invite them to move between work and a short stretch break. A fort that works for ten minutes is better than one forced for thirty.
