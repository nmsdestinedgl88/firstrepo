# MODULE 2 | Active Power | From High Energy to Active Power

## OPENER-1 | Meet the Power
Some children seem to run on a different battery. They climb the furniture, tap the table, hum while they eat and ask if you can go to the park five minutes after you got home from it.

If you are a parent of one of these children, you have probably heard the word "too." Too much, too loud, too wiggly. It is easy to start feeling that your child's energy is a problem to be managed.

Here is another way to see it. Energy is fuel. Athletes, performers, builders and entrepreneurs all rely on it. The same drive that bounces off the walls at 7 p.m. can power a long hike, a school play or a first job on a busy shift. Many parents notice their child seems to think better after moving, not just after burning off steam.

The trouble is not the energy itself. It is when and where it shows up. A classroom, a car seat and a dinner table all ask for stillness, and that can be a real stretch.

This module does not try to shrink your child's energy. It gives it better places to go. You will map when your child's energy peaks, build movement into the day and create a wind-down routine that lets the body settle.

For you, patience here means tolerating motion. You will practice letting a wiggle be a wiggle, and picking your battles about what truly needs to be still.

PULLQUOTE: Energy is not the enemy. It just needs a place to go.

## MISSION 2.1 | The Energy Map | 10 minutes a day for 3 days | Chart in this book

### The Idea
Before you plan anything, find out what is actually happening. Energy is rarely random. Most children have times when it peaks, times when it dips and moments when a small change makes a big difference.

For three days, jot down quick notes at set points in the day. You are looking for patterns, not perfection. A map like this helps you schedule hard tasks when your child is most ready and plan movement before the moments that ask for stillness.

Keep it light. Two minutes a time is plenty.

### Steps
1. Choose three check-in times, such as before school, after school and before bed.
2. At each time, rate your child's energy from 1 (low) to 5 (very high).
3. Note what happened just before and what helped or hurt.
4. After three days, look for patterns together if your child is willing.
5. Circle the best time for focus tasks and the best time for active play.

### Say This
> "I'm noticing when you have the most energy. When do you feel most ready to run?"
> "When do you feel most tired or grumpy?"

### Notice
- Times of day with high energy and good mood.
- Times when energy is high but things go badly.
- Effects of food, sleep and activity on the day.
- Surprising patterns.

### Guardian Pause
If a low-energy moment makes you worry, remind yourself that every child has them. Take a breath and record what you see without adding a story to it. Facts first, then conclusions.

### If It Isn't Working
If you forget to check in, set a phone reminder. If three days feels like too much, track one day to start. Even one day of notes tells you more than guessing.

## MISSION 2.2 | Move, Then Think | 10 minutes | Space to move

### The Idea
Many kids settle into a seated task better after moving first. Two to five minutes of active play before homework or a quiet activity can make sitting feel more doable.

This mission is a simple experiment. You will try a short burst of movement before a seated task and see what changes. Make it fun so it does not feel like a chore.

Keep the movement short and playful: jumping jacks, a dance, a lap around the garden, a game of animal walks.

### Steps
1. Pick a task that requires sitting, such as homework or reading.
2. Choose a two-to-five minute movement game together.
3. Move with your child. Your participation matters.
4. Take one slow breath together when the movement ends.
5. Start the task and notice how your child settles.

### Say This
> "Let's get our wiggles out first. Pick a move."
> "Ready? Big breath, then let's begin."

### Notice
- How long your child stays on task after movement.
- Which kind of movement works best.
- Whether the transition back to sitting is smooth.
- How you feel after moving too.

### Guardian Pause
Join in even if you feel silly. When you are doing star jumps beside a laughing child, patience is easier to find. Joy and calm are close neighbours.

### If It Isn't Working
If movement winds your child up instead of settling them, switch to slower options like stretching or pushing against a wall. Try different types until you find a good match.

## MISSION 2.3 | The Mission Circuit | 15 minutes | Household items

### The Idea
An indoor or outdoor obstacle course turns movement into a game. Add a learning task at each station, such as spelling a word, counting jumps or naming a feeling, and you combine energy with thinking.

Build the circuit from things you already have: cushions to hop between, a line of tape to walk, a chair to crawl under, a laundry basket to toss socks into. Let your child help design it.

Keep it safe, clear and short. Three to five stations is plenty.

### Steps
1. Choose a safe space and clear anything breakable.
2. Build three to five stations with your child.
3. Add one small challenge at each station.
4. Run the circuit together, timing the first run.
5. Let your child redesign it and go again.

### Say This
> "Let's build a mission course. What's the first challenge?"
> "How could we make it harder?"

### Notice
- Ideas your child comes up with.
- How they cope with waiting their turn.
- Signs of pride at finishing.
- How you handle noise and mess.

### Guardian Pause
A noisy circuit tests patience. Before you begin, decide what level of mess is acceptable, and say it out loud. Setting your limit in advance saves you from snapping in the middle.

### If It Isn't Working
If space is tight, use a hallway or a small corner and scale it down. If your child loses interest, switch to a timed challenge or add a prize like picking the next game.

## MISSION 2.4 | Big Helper Jobs | 10 minutes | A task to do

### The Idea
Some of the best ways to use energy are real jobs. Carrying, pushing, pulling and lifting tasks give a child's energy a purpose, and being needed builds confidence.

Choose jobs that suit your child's age and are safe: carrying groceries, wiping the table, watering plants, stacking firewood, pushing the laundry basket, taking out the recycling. Frame them as important missions, because they are.

The goal is not free labour. It is to show your child that their strength is useful.

### Steps
1. Choose two or three age-appropriate jobs together.
2. Explain why the job matters to the family.
3. Show how to do it safely, then step back.
4. Thank them specifically afterward: "You carried every bag. That saved me a lot of time."
5. Add a job to your weekly routine if it went well.

### Say This
> "I need a strong helper for a mission. Are you in?"
> "That was a big help. Thank you for carrying that."

### Notice
- Which jobs your child enjoys most.
- How they stand taller after being thanked.
- Whether doing a job helps them settle afterward.
- How it feels when you let them do it their way.

### Guardian Pause
Kids rarely do chores as perfectly as adults would. Take a breath and choose to value effort over finish. You can fix the corner later, privately, or not at all.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child resists, make it a team job and do it side by side. Turn on music or race a timer. If the job is too hard, scale it down until they win.

## MISSION 2.5 | The Wind-Down Ritual | 10 minutes | A quiet corner

### The Idea
After a day of high energy, bodies need help to slow down. A short, repeating routine in the same order every night signals that it is time to settle. Predictability is soothing, and it takes pressure off you to invent a new plan each evening.

Include a few slow steps: dimming lights, stretching, a few deep breaths, a quiet story. Let your child choose the order so it feels like theirs.

Practice during the day first so the routine is familiar when you need it.

### Steps
1. Choose three or four calming steps with your child.
2. Write or draw them in order on a card.
3. Try the sequence together, slowly.
4. Use the same cues each evening: lights lower, voices softer.
5. Praise any effort to slow down, even small.

### Say This
> "It's wind-down time. What's our first step?"
> "Let's take three slow breaths together."

### Notice
- Which steps help your child relax most.
- How long the wind-down takes.
- Changes in the mood of the evening.
- Whether you settle too.

### Guardian Pause
Evenings are tiring for you as well. Do the breathing with your child and let your shoulders drop. You are not just guiding them; you are resetting yourself.

### If It Isn't Working
If it takes too long, keep it to two steps for a week, then add more. If bedtime battles continue, focus on consistency over perfection and try again with fewer demands.
