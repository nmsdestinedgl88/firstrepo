# MODULE 3 | Spark Ideas | From Impulsive Thinking to Creative Thinking

## OPENER-1 | Meet the Power
Ideas arrive fast in your child's mind. One minute they are designing a rocket, the next they are inventing a new game, and before you can answer they have a third plan that involves the dog.

Quick, jumping thoughts can be exhausting for parents. They can also be the raw material of creativity. Many creative people describe minds that make unusual connections and see possibilities others miss. Your child's brain may be doing exactly that.

The same speed has a downside. Acting on an idea before thinking it through can lead to spills, arguments, broken things and hurt feelings. Blurting out answers, interrupting and diving in without a plan are common for kids who think fast.

This module does not aim to slow the ideas down. It teaches a three-part habit: spark, plan, go. Your child keeps the creativity and adds a short pause before action. You will practice the pause through games, so it feels like play rather than correction.

For you, patience means welcoming the flood of ideas without feeling you must act on each one. You can say "Great idea, let's save it" and still keep your evening on track.

PULLQUOTE: A fast mind needs a good catcher's mitt, not a brake.

## MISSION 3.1 | The Idea Jar | 10 minutes | A jar, paper, pens

### The Idea
When ideas are valued, kids share them. When they are brushed aside, kids stop offering. An Idea Jar gives every spark a home so it is never lost, and so you can say "yes, save it" instead of "not now."

Decorate a jar together. Whenever your child has an idea, they write or draw it on a slip of paper and drop it in. Once a week, pull out a few slips and choose one to try.

The jar teaches an important skill: ideas can wait without disappearing.

### Steps
1. Find a jar or box and let your child decorate it.
2. Cut paper into small slips and keep a pen nearby.
3. Explain: "When you have an idea, we put it in the jar so it stays safe."
4. Write down the first idea together right away.
5. Once a week, choose one to try as a family.

### Say This
> "That's a great idea. Let's put it in the jar so we don't lose it."
> "Which idea from the jar should we try this weekend?"

### Notice
- How your child reacts when an idea gets saved.
- The kinds of ideas that appear.
- Whether waiting feels easier with the jar.
- The ideas that you can actually make happen.

### Guardian Pause
When your child shares an idea at an inconvenient moment, take a slow breath before you reply. "Jar it" can become a calm phrase for both of you.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child forgets to use the jar, keep slips and a pen in the car or by the table. If the jar overflows, pick three favourites and let the rest go without guilt.

## MISSION 3.2 | Yes, And Stories | 10 minutes | Imagination

### The Idea
Building a story together is a playful way to practice listening, turn-taking and building on another person's ideas. The rule is simple: every contribution starts with "Yes, and..."

Fast thinkers love this game because their ideas are welcome. They also practice waiting for their turn and connecting their thought to someone else's, which makes conversations easier.

Keep it silly. A story about a dragon who is afraid of homework is a perfect start.

### Steps
1. Choose a starting sentence, such as "One day a very small elephant found a key."
2. Take turns adding one sentence each, starting with "Yes, and..."
3. Keep going for five to ten minutes.
4. If your child interrupts, gently signal it is your turn and thank them for waiting.
5. End by reading the story back and choosing a title.

### Say This
> "Yes, and then the elephant opened the door."
> "I loved your idea. My turn to add one."

### Notice
- How your child handles waiting for their turn.
- Whether they build on your idea or replace it.
- Funny moments you want to remember.
- How you feel being playful together.

### Guardian Pause
Let the story go in an odd direction. You are not editing; you are playing. Notice any urge to correct, take a breath and say "Yes, and..."

### If It Isn't Working
If turns are hard, use a toy as a talking stick: only the person holding it speaks. If the story stalls, ask a question like "What happens next?"

## MISSION 3.3 | Stop-Think-Go | 10 minutes | Open space

### The Idea
A pause is a skill, and skills grow through practice. This game works like a traffic light: green means move, yellow means slow down and red means freeze. Playing it builds the body-brain habit of stopping before acting.

Play it as a game with no pressure and plenty of laughter. Take turns being the light so your child gets to control you too.

Over time you can use the same words in real life: "Yellow light. What's the plan?"

### Steps
1. Explain the colours: green is go, yellow is slow, red is stop.
2. Call colours while your child moves around the room.
3. Add silly versions: green means hop, yellow means tiptoe.
4. Swap roles and let your child be the traffic light.
5. After a few rounds, ask: "What did you notice about stopping fast?"

### Say This
> "Green light! Go go go."
> "Yellow light. Slow your body down."

### Notice
- How quickly your child responds to "red."
- Whether they enjoy being the light.
- Moments they stop without being told.
- Any frustration and how you respond.

### Guardian Pause
When you are the one who is moving, you also practice stopping. Pay attention to how it feels to freeze on "red." That small pause is the same one you use before you speak sharply.

### If It Isn't Working
If it feels babyish, make it a faster game with a timer or add silly challenges. For older kids, use it as a "pause button" joke in everyday life.

## MISSION 3.4 | The Build-It Challenge | 15 minutes | Household items

### The Idea
Building with ordinary items gives your child's creativity somewhere real to go. Hand over a pile of boxes, tape, cups and paper, and set a simple challenge: build a bridge, a tower, a boat that floats or a marble run.

The best part comes after the build. Ask what worked, what failed and what they would change. This is the heart of invention: try, test, fix. It also shows that mistakes are part of making things.

Keep your hands back and let your child lead.

### Steps
1. Gather safe materials such as cardboard, tape, cups and string.
2. Choose a challenge, such as "Build something that can hold a toy car."
3. Set a timer for ten minutes and let your child build.
4. Test the creation together.
5. Ask: "What would you change next time?"

### Say This
> "What are you building? Tell me about it."
> "What do you want to try differently?"

### Notice
- How your child reacts when something doesn't work.
- Creative solutions that surprise you.
- Whether they plan before building or jump right in.
- How hard it is for you not to help.

### Guardian Pause
If the structure collapses, resist the urge to fix it or say "I told you so." Take a slow breath and say, "Interesting. What happened?"

### If It Isn't Working
If your child gets frustrated, shrink the challenge or build alongside them. If they lose interest, switch to a new material or let them choose the challenge.

## MISSION 3.5 | The Two-Minute Plan | 10 minutes | Paper, pen

### The Idea
Spark, plan, go. The two-minute plan sits in the middle. Before your child starts a new idea, they spend two minutes naming three steps and the things they will need. Two minutes is short enough to feel doable and long enough to catch problems.

Start with something fun, like baking cookies or building a den. Make the plan together, then follow it. Afterwards, ask whether the plan helped.

Over time, the habit can spread to bigger tasks, such as homework and cleaning.

### Steps
1. Choose a project your child is excited about.
2. Ask: "What are the first three steps?" Write them down.
3. Ask: "What will you need?" List the items.
4. Set a two-minute timer to gather supplies.
5. Do the project, then talk about how the plan helped.

### Say This
> "Great idea. What are the first three steps?"
> "What do we need before we start?"

### Notice
- Whether the plan saves time or frustration.
- How easily your child makes the plan.
- Steps they skip or add.
- How the project felt compared with last time.

### Guardian Pause
If your child resists the plan, take a breath. Remind yourself that you are teaching a habit, not controlling the outcome. Try again next time with a lighter touch.

### If It Isn't Working
If planning feels like a chore, draw it as a comic strip with three panels. If your child jumps in before the plan, laugh, reset and try again.
