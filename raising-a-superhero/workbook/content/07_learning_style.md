# MODULE 7 | Learning Style | From Restless Learner to Confident Learner

## OPENER-1 | Meet the Power
School can be the hardest part of the day. Sitting still, copying from the board, finishing worksheets and waiting for turns all ask for the very skills that come least naturally to many kids with ADHD.

At home, that difficulty can arrive in the form of homework battles. Tears, bargaining and a pencil that has gone missing three times can turn a twenty-minute task into a two-hour standoff.

Yet the same children often learn brilliantly in other ways. They may remember everything about a topic they love. They may learn best by doing, moving, drawing, talking or teaching. Their curiosity may run far beyond the curriculum. A restless learner is often an active one.

This module focuses on finding how your child learns best and building homework and school routines around it. You will test different approaches, set up a simple homework launch and build a team with your child's teacher.

Nothing here replaces the support your child's school and professionals can offer. Think of these missions as practical home experiments that give you something helpful to share with them.

For you, patience means staying out of the power struggle. Your job is to set up the conditions and stay calm. Your child's job is to do the learning. When you hold both lightly, homework gets easier.

PULLQUOTE: A restless learner is often an active learner. Find the doorway that fits.

## MISSION 7.1 | Learning Style Test Drive | 20 minutes | A short list to learn

### The Idea
Not every child learns well by reading or copying. This mission is a friendly experiment: try learning the same small list in three different ways and see which feels best.

Use something real, like five spelling words or five facts from school. Let your child try saying it out loud, moving while repeating it, and drawing it. Then ask which method felt easiest.

You are not looking for a label. You are collecting clues about what helps your child take information in.

### Steps
1. Choose a short list of five items to learn.
2. Try method one: say and repeat each item out loud.
3. Try method two: move or act out each item.
4. Try method three: draw a quick picture for each item.
5. Test your child on the list and ask which way felt best.

### Say This
> "Let's try three ways to learn these and see which one works for you."
> "Which way made it easiest to remember?"

### Notice
- Which method led to the best recall.
- Which method your child enjoyed most.
- Whether they asked to repeat any method.
- How you feel being the helper rather than the teacher.

### Guardian Pause
If your child gets some items wrong, skip the correction speech. Say "Good try, let's look at that one together." Your calm tone keeps their confidence up.

### If It Isn't Working
If all methods feel hard, try shorter lists or add a fourth method such as singing or tapping a rhythm. Different tasks may suit different methods, so test again another day.

## MISSION 7.2 | The Homework Launch | 15 minutes | Snack, water, a timer

### The Idea
The first few minutes of homework are the hardest. A launch routine eases the start. It is a short, repeatable sequence that happens before any work: a snack, a drink, a quick movement and a clear plan.

Doing the same things in the same order every day makes it easier to begin without a fight. It also helps you avoid starting with "Have you done your homework?" which often triggers resistance.

Design the launch together and keep it brief.

### Steps
1. Choose a regular homework time, with a short break after school first.
2. Agree on launch steps: snack, water, two-minute stretch, set up the space.
3. Write them on a card in order.
4. Look at the homework together and decide what to do first.
5. Start a timer and begin.

### Say This
> "Launch time. What's the first step on your card?"
> "Let's pick the first task together."

### Notice
- How long the launch takes.
- Whether starting feels easier.
- Which launch steps matter most.
- How you handle the urge to hover.

### Guardian Pause
Once your child begins, step back. Sit nearby with your own task. If you feel the urge to correct, hold it for a moment. A calm presence can say more than a comment.

### If It Isn't Working
If the launch drags out, trim it to two steps. If your child is exhausted after school, try a later time or a longer break first. Be flexible while keeping the order the same.

## MISSION 7.3 | Chunk and Choose | 10 minutes | Homework, a pencil, sticky notes

### The Idea
A big pile of homework looks endless. Chunking splits it into small pieces, and choosing gives your child some control over the order. Control reduces resistance.

Break the work into chunks of five to ten minutes, write each on a sticky note and let your child arrange them. Which one first? Which one is the most fun? Which gets saved for last? They peel off each note as they finish.

The visible progress is motivating, and the choices build planning skills.

### Steps
1. Look at the homework together.
2. Split it into chunks of about ten minutes.
3. Put each chunk on its own sticky note.
4. Let your child choose the order.
5. Remove each note as it is finished and take a short break between chunks.

### Say This
> "Let's break this into pieces. Which would you like to start with?"
> "One chunk down. Take a quick break."

### Notice
- The order your child chooses.
- Whether choice reduces resistance.
- How they respond to peeling off the notes.
- Chunks that need to be smaller.

### Guardian Pause
Accept an order that differs from yours. If they pick the hardest task first, great. If they pick the easiest, that works too. What matters is that they start.

### If It Isn't Working
If your child cannot decide, offer two choices only. If they rush through to peel off notes, add a quick check step: "Show me one answer you're proud of."

## MISSION 7.4 | Teacher Team-Up | 20 minutes | Paper or email

### The Idea
Your child's teacher sees a side of your child that you do not, and you see a side the teacher does not. Sharing that knowledge helps everyone. A short, friendly note that starts with strengths builds a partnership.

Write the note together with your child if they are willing. Share what you have learned in this book, such as what motivates them and which supports have helped at home. Ask what the teacher sees, and how you can work together.

This note is not a request for a diagnosis or a medical opinion. It is a conversation between people who care about the same child.

### Steps
1. List three strengths and interests you know about your child.
2. List two things that have helped at home.
3. Write a short note that shares both and asks what the teacher has noticed.
4. Ask whether you can meet or talk for ten minutes.
5. Follow up with thanks.

### Say This
> "I'd love to hear what you see in class, and share what's working at home."
> "Thank you for helping us support our child."

### Notice
- How the teacher responds.
- New ideas that come from the conversation.
- How your child feels about you and the teacher working together.
- What you can try at home as a result.

### Guardian Pause
If the conversation touches on worries, breathe and listen first. You can take notes and reply later. You do not need to solve everything in the meeting.

### If It Isn't Working
If you do not hear back, try again with a short message or ask the school office who is the best contact. If the relationship is strained, ask whether another member of staff could join the conversation.

## MISSION 7.5 | The Brain Break Menu | 15 minutes | Paper, markers

### The Idea
Breaks work best when they are planned. A Brain Break Menu is a list of quick, two-minute activities your child can choose between homework chunks. Because they chose the options, it feels more like a treat than an interruption.

Make the menu together and decorate it. Include movement, calming and fun choices: jumping jacks, drawing a doodle, a drink and stretch, a quick song, a silly dance, a few slow breaths.

Set a timer so breaks stay short.

### Steps
1. Brainstorm break ideas with your child.
2. Choose six that take two minutes or less and need no screen.
3. Write or draw them on a menu and put it where homework happens.
4. Use it between chunks, letting your child pick.
5. Set a timer for two minutes and return to work when it rings.

### Say This
> "Time for a brain break. What's on the menu?"
> "Two minutes. Then we're back for the next chunk."

### Notice
- Which breaks your child picks.
- Whether they return to work easily.
- Breaks that make things harder to restart.
- How your child feels about the menu.

### Guardian Pause
Take a brain break yourself. A quick stretch or a sip of water between chunks resets your patience, too. You are modelling the habit.

### If It Isn't Working
If breaks stretch out, shorten them or use a visual timer. If your child hates stopping, let them decide when to break within a set number of chunks.
